Le quattro domande (the four questions)

The question decides the form of the answer.

Ask someone (tu)You answer (io)Ask about someone (lui/lei)You answer

What's your name?		Mi chiamo…Come si chiama?Si chiama…

How are you?		Sto…Come sta?Sta…

Say Piacere! ("nice to meet you") when you meet someone, and drop the subject pronoun. Sono di Boston, not Io sono di Boston. The verb ending already says who you mean.

Di dov'è? is Di dove è? with the e of dove dropped. È with the accent means "is"; e without it means "and".

A full introduction: Piacere! Mi chiamo Justin. Sono di ______. Studio ______. Oggi sto ______.

What drives the forms:

  • Studiare is regular. For io, tu, lui/lei, every regular -are verb ends -o, -i, -a.
  • Essere and stare are irregular. Their forms follow no pattern.
  • Chiamarsi is reflexive, so two things change: the pronoun in front (mi, ti, si, ci, vi, si) and the ending.

Come stai? Le risposte (how you're doing)

Best to worst:

  1. benissimo / molto bene great / very well
  2. bene well
  3. così così so-so
  4. non bene not well
  5. male badly

Add oggi ("today") to say how you are right now: Oggi sto benissimo!

Pronuncia: la regola dura/morbida (the hard/soft rule)

The rule governs c, g, and sc.

Before a, o, u the sound is hard. Before e, i it is soft.

To keep it hard in front of e or i, Italian inserts an h: che, chi, ghe, ghi, sche, schi. To make it soft in front of a, o, u, it inserts an i: cia, cio, ciu, gia, gio, giu, scia, scio, sciu. That inserted letter is silent; it only sets the sound.

I saluti (how you are)

AskingEnglish
Come stai?how are you? (informal)
Come sta?how are you? (formal)
Come va?how's it going?

Answers follow Sto… ("I'm…"), best to worst:

  1. benone terrific
  2. benissimo / molto bene very well
  3. abbastanza bene quite well
  4. bene fine
  5. così così so-so
  6. male badly

Two answers stand outside that list: Non c'è male ("not too bad") and Non sto bene ("I'm not well"). To return the question, say Bene, grazie, e tu? or, formally, Bene, grazie, e Lei?

Masculine titles ending in -ore drop the final e before a name: signore becomes signor Bianchi, professore becomes professor Ricci. Feminine titles do not change. A title can also stand on its own as a greeting: Buongiorno, Avvocato!

Three rules govern the spelling:

  • Numbers are written as a single word.
  • Venti, trenta, quaranta and the rest drop their final vowel before uno and otto, which start with vowels: ventuno, ventotto, trentuno.
  • From 23 on, tre takes an accent at the end of a number: ventitré, trentatré.

Note culturali (culture and usage)

  • A handshake is the standard greeting. Friends kiss on both cheeks. Ciao comes from the Venetian dialect word schiavo, "slave", used in the sense of "I am your servant".
  • Titles are used far more than in English, often on their own where English would use a name.
  • Italian is an official language in Italy, Switzerland, San Marino, and Vatican City, with large communities in Canada, the United States, Germany, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, and Venezuela.

Prestiti (loanwords)

Italian keeps words from other languages unchanged, which makes them free vocabulary. From Latin: ultimatum, agenda, curriculum. From French: chef, chalet, élite, buffet. From English: stress, leader, show, business, part-time, fitness. The letters j, k, w, x, y turn up almost only in words like these.
